The [National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/caregiving/long-distance-caregiving) describes a long-distance caregiver as anyone who lives an hour or more away from the person they help. For many families it is much further: another state, another time zone, another country.

I built I Love My Parents after years of checking on my own parents from another country, and most of what follows I learned the slow way. The good news is that distance changes how you care, not how much you can do.

## The real challenges of long-distance caregiving

- **You hear about problems late.** A fall, a missed appointment or a new symptom often reaches you days later, and in a softened version.
- **"I'm fine" is the default answer.** Parents do not want to worry you, especially on the phone.
- **Every trip costs time and money.** So visits get saved for emergencies, which is exactly when they are most stressful.
- **The load falls unevenly.** The sibling who lives closest often does most of the work, and resentment builds on both sides.
- **Guilt.** Almost every long-distance caregiver feels they should be doing more.

None of this goes away completely. A bit of structure makes all of it smaller.

## Step 1: Find out what is really going on

Use your next visit to look, not just to catch up. Notice the fridge, the post, the medicines and how your parent moves around the home and manages the stairs. Our [checklist for an elderly parent living alone](/blog/checklist-for-elderly-parent-living-alone/) walks you through it room by room.

Also ask your parent directly what has become harder. People often admit to a small difficulty, such as opening jars or reading letters, long before a big one.

## Step 2: Build a local team

Distance is manageable when someone nearby can be at the door within half an hour. Make a short list of people and what each of them can do:

- A neighbour who has a spare key and your number.
- A friend or relative nearby who is happy to pop round.
- Their doctor and pharmacist, who know the medicines and the history.
- A local care service or geriatric care manager, if needs grow beyond what the family can cover.

Thank these people often. A neighbour who feels appreciated is far more likely to knock when the curtains stay closed.

## Step 3: Get the paperwork in place

Paperwork is the part of caregiving you can do from anywhere, and the hardest to sort out in a crisis. While your parent is well, talk about:

- Written permission for their doctor to speak to you about their health.
- A healthcare proxy or advance directive that says who decides if they cannot. In England and Wales this is a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare.
- A durable power of attorney for money and property.
- A list of accounts, insurance policies and bills, and where the important documents are kept.

The rules differ by state and country, so ask an elder law attorney (a solicitor in the UK) to check the documents.

## Step 4: Agree on a daily signal and a plan

When you live far away, the scariest question is how long something could go unnoticed. The answer should be: not long. Agree with your parent on one small daily signal that tells the family the day has started normally, and on what happens if it does not come.

## Step 5: Make visits count

- Book doctor's appointments for the days you are there, with your parent's agreement, and go along.
- Keep a running list of jobs between visits: the smoke alarm, the gutter, a new phone.
- Stock up on things that are heavy or hard to buy: water, cleaning products, batteries.
- Leave time that has nothing to do with caregiving. You are still their child, not their case manager.

## Step 6: Share the load with siblings

Caregiving goes better when each person owns a part of it, rather than everyone helping a little with everything. Split it by strengths, not only by distance:

- **Health:** appointments, medicines and talking to doctors.
- **Money:** bills, benefits, insurance and paperwork.
- **Home:** repairs, shopping and the local team.
- **Company:** regular calls, visits and keeping your parent's social life going.

Keep shared notes that everyone can see, and agree on one family chat for updates so nobody hears news second hand.

## Long-distance caregiving tips at a glance

1. Know the medicines, the doctor and the pharmacy by name. A [printable medication chart](/blog/free-printable-medication-chart-for-elderly/) helps.
2. Keep a spare key with someone local.
3. Put the paperwork in place before you need it.
4. Agree on a daily signal and a written plan for when it does not come.
5. Call for conversation, not only to check.
6. Use video calls to see how your parent looks and moves, not only how they sound.
7. Plan visits around appointments and jobs, then leave room for fun.
8. Split roles with siblings and keep shared notes.
9. Learn about local services before a crisis, not during one.
10. Look after yourself, so you can keep doing this for years.

## Look after yourself too

Long-distance caregiving can last for many years. Set a pace you can keep, accept help when it is offered, and talk to someone, a friend or a support group, about how it feels. A calm, rested you is worth more to your parent than an exhausted one who calls ten times a day.
