"Elderly monitoring system" covers everything from a button on a necklace to sensors that quietly learn your parent's daily routine. Before you compare brands, decide what you are actually worried about: a fall, a whole day with no news, wandering at night, or simply not knowing how they are. The right system follows from the worry, not the other way round.

The worry about falls is well founded. The CDC says [more than one in four older adults fall each year](https://www.cdc.gov/falls/data-research/facts-stats/index.html), and fewer than half of them tell their doctor. But a fall is only one of the things families want to know about, and no single device covers everything.

## The six types of elderly monitoring systems

### 1. Medical alert buttons

A pendant or wristband with a button. Press it and you are connected to a monitoring center that can talk to your parent, call family and send an ambulance. Home versions work through a base station. Mobile versions use a cellular network and GPS, so they also work outside.

- **Good at:** getting help fast when your parent can press the button.
- **Misses:** anything that happens when they are unconscious, confused or not wearing it. Many people take the pendant off at night or in the shower, exactly when falls happen.
- **Cost model:** a device plus a monthly monitoring fee.

### 2. Fall detection wearables and smartwatches

Some alert pendants and smartwatches detect a hard fall automatically. The Apple Watch, for example, can call emergency services if it detects a hard fall and the wearer does not respond.

- **Good at:** falls where your parent cannot call for help.
- **Misses:** soft or slow falls, like sliding off a chair. It only works if the watch is worn and charged, which for a smartwatch means charging most days.
- **Cost model:** the device, and sometimes a cellular plan or a monitoring subscription.

### 3. Home sensors

Motion sensors, door sensors, bed sensors and smart plugs. Over a week or two they learn the normal day: when your parent gets up, uses the kitchen, goes to bed. If the pattern breaks, for example no movement by 10am, the family gets an alert.

- **Good at:** noticing that something is off without your parent wearing or pressing anything.
- **Misses:** the exact moment of a fall. Alerts come after a gap in activity, and pets or visitors can confuse them.
- **Cost model:** a starter kit, sometimes with a subscription for alerts.

### 4. Camera-free fall sensors

A newer kind of device that uses radar or other sensors to detect a fall in a room without filming anything. It is mounted on a wall like a smoke alarm.

- **Good at:** bathrooms and bedrooms, where cameras are out of the question and wearables come off.
- **Misses:** rooms without a sensor. You need one per room you care about, and they cost more than basic sensors.
- **Cost model:** a device per room, often with a subscription.

### 5. Cameras

A video monitoring system for the elderly at home lets you see a room live, and some cameras detect falls or allow two-way talk.

- **Good at:** seeing what is happening when you are worried.
- **Misses:** privacy. Many parents refuse cameras outright, and they are never acceptable in a bathroom or bedroom. A camera only helps if someone is watching or alerts are set up.
- **Cost model:** cheap hardware, with cloud storage often on subscription.

### 6. Daily check-in apps

Your parent confirms once a day that they are fine, usually with one tap. If the confirmation does not come, the app reminds them and then tells the family.

- **Good at:** making sure a bad day is noticed within hours, not days. It costs little and leaves your parent in control.
- **Misses:** anything that happens between check-ins. It is not an emergency service and should not replace one for a parent at high risk of falls.
- **Cost model:** often free, with paid extras.

## How to choose the right system for your parent

Start from your parent's situation, not from a product list.

- **They have fallen before, or are frail and live alone.** A fall detection wearable with a monitoring center, plus home safety changes. Pick a waterproof device so it stays on in the shower.
- **They are independent and healthy, but live alone.** A daily check-in and one local person with a key is often enough. Add an alert button later if things change.
- **They have memory problems or wander.** Door sensors and a GPS device, and a conversation with their doctor about how safe living alone still is.
- **They refuse to wear anything.** Home sensors or a camera-free fall sensor in the bathroom, together with a daily check-in.
- **You live far away.** You need both a daily signal and someone nearby who can get there fast. Our guide to [long-distance caregiving](/blog/long-distance-caregiving/) covers the rest.

Whatever you choose, make the home itself safer first. Grab bars, good lighting and no loose rugs prevent the falls that monitoring only reports. Our [checklist for an elderly parent living alone](/blog/checklist-for-elderly-parent-living-alone/) goes through it room by room.

## Questions to ask before you buy

- Does it need wifi, a landline or mobile coverage? Check coverage at your parent's actual address, in the rooms that matter.
- Who gets the alert: a staffed monitoring center, or family members' phones? If it is family, who answers at 3am?
- What is the monthly fee, the minimum contract and the cancellation process?
- How often does it need charging, and is it waterproof?
- How many false alarms should you expect, and how are they cancelled?
- Who can see the data, and can your parent pause it or see what you see?

## Talk to your parent before you set anything up

The best system is the one your parent agrees to. Monitoring that is installed without a real conversation often ends up unplugged, left in a drawer or resented.

Frame it as the thing that lets them keep living at home, not as a sign that they cannot cope. Let them choose between two or three options. Be open about what you will see and what you will not. A parent who knows that you only get an alert when something is off usually feels much less watched than one who suspects you are looking all the time.

## Most families end up combining two layers

In practice, a single device rarely covers everything. The setup that works for many families has two layers:

1. **An everyday layer** that tells you the day has started normally: a check-in app, home sensors or a regular call.
2. **An emergency layer** for the moment something goes wrong: an alert button or fall detection device, plus a neighbour or relative who can get there quickly.

The everyday layer stops you worrying about ordinary days. The emergency layer is there for the rare bad one. If you want a gentle way to set up the everyday layer, read [how to check on an elderly parent every day](/blog/how-to-check-on-elderly-parent-daily/) without it turning into nagging.
