When a parent takes three or four medicines, memory is usually enough. At six or eight, spread across the day, it stops being enough for anyone. A written chart answers the two questions every family worries about: did they take it, and did they take it twice?

How to fill in a medication tracker for your parent

  1. Collect every bottle and box in one place, including vitamins, supplements, creams, inhalers and anything bought without a prescription.
  2. Copy each medicine onto page 2 exactly as the label says: the name, the strength and how many to take.
  3. Group the doses by time of day on page 1. Morning pills go in one row, evening pills in another, so your parent ticks a group rather than ten separate lines.
  4. Note anything that matters for timing: with food, on an empty stomach, not with milk.
  5. Ask the pharmacist to check the finished list. It takes a few minutes, and they can spot doubles and clashes.
  6. Put the chart where the pills live, with a pen tied next to it.

Why grouping pills by time of day works

Most medication tracker templates list medicines one by one. That looks tidy on paper, but it is not how anybody takes pills. People take them in batches: a handful with breakfast, a couple with dinner.

A chart built around those moments matches the routine your parent already has. There are fewer boxes to tick, it lines up with a weekly pill organiser, and a missed batch stands out at a glance.

Tips that make a paper medication chart stick

  • Tie the habit to something that already happens every day, such as the morning kettle or the evening news.
  • Keep the chart, the pen and the pill organiser in the same spot.
  • Start a new sheet every Sunday and keep the old ones. A month of charts is useful at the next doctor's appointment.
  • For a medicine taken only now and then, like a painkiller, write the time in the box instead of a tick.
  • Update page 2 the same day a medicine changes, and cross out the old line rather than writing over it.
  • Take the list to every appointment, and to the hospital if it ever comes to that.
Please note. The chart helps you keep track. It is not medical advice. Never change, stop or start a medicine without talking to the doctor or pharmacist.

Medication reminders for the elderly: paper, alarm or app?

A printable medication tracker is enough when your parent is organised, the routine is simple and someone in the family sees the chart often. It gets harder when you live far away: a paper chart cannot tell you that the evening pills were missed until your next visit.

Simple reminder ideas that work alongside the chart:

  • A daily phone alarm named after the batch, for example "Evening pills".
  • A weekly pill organiser with a compartment for each time of day.
  • Blister packs made up by the pharmacy, already sorted by day and time.
  • A reminder app that also shows the family which doses were taken.

Questions people ask

Is the medication chart really free?

Yes. Download the PDF and print as many copies as you need. There is no sign-up and no email.

What should a daily medication chart for an elderly person include?

The name and strength of each medicine, how many to take, the time of day, instructions such as with food, and a box to tick for each dose. A separate list with what each medicine is for, who prescribed it and the pharmacy is useful for appointments and emergencies.

Should vitamins and supplements go on the chart?

Yes. Supplements can interact with prescription medicines, so the doctor and pharmacist need to see them on the list too.

How do I print the chart on A4 paper?

Choose "Fit to page" in the print settings. The chart is made for US Letter and scales to A4 without losing anything.

Is a paper chart or an app better for medication reminders?

Paper is simple and works well when someone sees it every day. An app adds reminders at the right time and lets family members far away see missed doses. Many families use both.
