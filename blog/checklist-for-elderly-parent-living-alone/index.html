A checklist for an elderly parent living alone

Most parents want to stay in their own home for as long as they can. This checklist helps you make that home safer, agree on a simple daily routine and notice early when living alone stops working.

By Andrii Nalyvaiko · November 20, 2026 · 7 min read

In 2023, about one in four Americans aged 65 and older lived alone, according to the Pew Research Center. For most of them it is a choice, not a problem. The home is familiar, the neighbours know them, and every cupboard is where it has always been. Your job is not to take that away. It is to remove the small risks that turn an ordinary fall or a missed pill into a crisis.

How to use this checklist

Go through it together on your next visit, not as an inspection while your parent watches. Ask "would this help you?" rather than "this is dangerous". Fix the easy things the same day: a bulb, a loose rug, a phone charger by the bed. Write down the rest and agree who does what.

Repeat it once a year, and after any fall, hospital stay, new diagnosis or change in medicines. Needs change slowly, and a yearly walk-through catches what you stop noticing.

Home safety checklist, room by room

Falls are the main reason older people living alone lose their independence. The CDC says more than one in four older adults fall each year, and fewer than half of them tell their doctor. Most of the fixes below cost little and take an afternoon.

Floors, stairs and lighting

  • Loose rugs are removed or held down with non-slip tape.
  • Cables and cords run along the walls, not across walkways.
  • Every staircase has a sturdy handrail, ideally on both sides.
  • Stairs and hallways are well lit, with a switch at the top and the bottom.
  • Night lights mark the way from the bedroom to the bathroom.
  • Things used every day sit between waist and shoulder height, so nobody needs a step stool.

Bathroom

  • Grab bars by the toilet and in the shower or bath. A towel rail is not a grab bar.
  • Non-slip mats inside and outside the shower.
  • A shower chair or bath seat if standing for long is tiring.
  • A raised toilet seat if getting up is hard.
  • Hot water set to a safe temperature, around 120°F (49°C).
  • The bathroom door can be opened from the outside.

Kitchen

  • A working smoke alarm, tested on every visit.
  • A stove with an automatic shut-off, or the habit of setting a timer.
  • A fire blanket or small extinguisher within reach.
  • Heavy pots and everyday plates on the lower shelves.
  • Easy meals in the freezer for days when cooking feels like too much.

Bedroom and night time

  • A lamp that can be switched on from the bed.
  • A charged phone within reach of the bed every night.
  • A carbon monoxide alarm if the home has gas, oil or a fireplace.
  • A bed low enough that both feet rest flat on the floor when sitting on the edge.

Front door and outside

  • A spare key with a trusted neighbour or in a lock box, not under the mat.
  • A house number that is easy to read from the street, so an ambulance can find it at night.
  • Outside steps that are lit, even and clear of leaves, snow and ice.
  • A door viewer or video doorbell to see who is there before opening.

Health and medication checklist

  • An up-to-date list of every medicine, dose and time, including vitamins and pills bought without a prescription. Our free printable medication chart has a page for exactly this.
  • A weekly pill organiser or pharmacy blister packs when there are several medicines.
  • A yearly medication review with the doctor or pharmacist.
  • Glasses and hearing aids checked, because poor sight and hearing make everything else harder.
  • The numbers for the doctor, the pharmacy and the family written down by the phone, not only saved in it.
  • A copy of the medicine list and insurance details where a paramedic would look, such as on the fridge.

Daily contact and emergencies checklist

For a parent who lives alone, this part of the checklist matters most. If something happens, how long until someone notices?

  • One small daily signal that tells the family the day has started normally.
  • A clear plan for what happens if that signal does not come, written down while nothing is wrong.
  • A way to call for help from anywhere in the home, not only from the phone in the hall.
  • At least one person nearby who can be at the door within half an hour.
  • Everyone in the family knows who calls whom.

Money, paperwork and scams checklist

People who live alone are a favourite target for phone and doorstep scams. A few agreements now save a lot of stress later.

  • Regular bills paid automatically, so nothing is cut off after a missed letter.
  • One simple rule: no payments, bank details or passwords over the phone, whoever is calling.
  • Bank alerts for large or unusual payments, with a second person on the alerts if your parent agrees.
  • Important documents kept in one folder, and someone in the family knows where it is.
  • A power of attorney and a healthcare proxy talked through and in place.

Social life and wellbeing checklist

Living alone and being lonely are not the same thing, but one can slide into the other quietly.

  • A reason to leave the house most days: a walk, the shops, a club, church or a class.
  • Regular calls that are just for talking, not for checking.
  • A neighbour or friend they see every week.
  • A plan for getting around when driving stops: a bus pass, a taxi account or a lift rota.
  • Something to look forward to on the calendar.

When an elderly parent insists on living alone

Sooner or later many families have this conversation. You see the risks. Your parent sees their home, their routine and their freedom. Both of you are right.

A few things make the talk easier:

  • Ask what living at home means to them and what would make them feel safer. Start from their goals, not your fears.
  • Agree on specific signs that would mean it is time to think again, so the decision is never about one bad week.
  • Offer changes in small steps: grab bars before carers, a daily check-in before a move.
  • Bring in a third voice, such as their doctor, if you keep going round in circles.

Respecting the choice does not mean doing nothing. Most of this checklist exists so that your parent can keep living alone for longer, with less risk.

Signs that living alone is no longer working

Watch for patterns rather than single events. Any of these, and especially several together, is a reason to talk to their doctor and look at more support:

  • Falls, near falls or bruises they cannot explain.
  • Weight loss, an empty fridge or food going off.
  • Missed medicines, or pills left over at the end of the week.
  • Unpaid bills, unopened post or new scam calls.
  • Getting lost on familiar routes, or confusion about the day.
  • The home, the laundry or personal hygiene slipping.
  • Pulling away from friends and activities they used to enjoy.

Questions people ask

How do I know if my elderly parent can still live alone?

Look at whether they can manage everyday tasks safely: washing, cooking, taking medicines, paying bills and getting help in an emergency. If those mostly work and the home is set up well, living alone is usually fine. Repeated falls, confusion, weight loss or missed medicines are signs to talk to their doctor about more support.

What should be on a checklist for elderly living alone?

Cover five areas: home safety (floors, bathroom, kitchen, lighting), health and medicines, daily contact and emergencies, money and scams, and social life. Daily contact matters most, because it decides how fast someone notices when something goes wrong.

How can I check on an elderly parent living alone every day?

Agree on one small daily signal, such as a text, a call at a set time or one tap in a check-in app, and decide in advance what happens if it does not come. A signal is quicker for your parent than a daily "are you OK?" call, and it tells you more.

What can I do if my elderly parent insists on living alone?

Start from what home means to them and what would make them feel safer. Agree on clear signs that would mean it is time to think again, make the home safer in small steps and set up a daily check-in. If you keep disagreeing, ask their doctor to join the conversation.

How often should we go through the checklist?

Once a year, and again after a fall, a hospital stay, a new diagnosis or a change in medicines.
